Beetroot Nitrates and Circulation: How Dietary Nitrate Works

Why a humble root vegetable became one of the most-studied nutrition topics in sport science.

By the Spartamax Editorial Team · Updated November 23, 2026

Beetroot went from salad garnish to a subject of sports science research in about a decade. The reason is dietary nitrate, a compound found in high amounts in beets and leafy greens.

From Nitrate to Nitric Oxide

When you eat nitrate-rich vegetables, bacteria on your tongue convert some of the nitrate to nitrite. The body can then turn nitrite into nitric oxide. Nitric oxide is a signaling molecule that helps blood vessels relax, and it is involved in how muscles use oxygen.

What the Research Shows

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements’ fact sheet on exercise and athletic performance has a dedicated section on beetroot. A 2024 meta-analysis of 27 studies in healthy men found that beetroot-based supplements produced modest improvements in repetitions to failure and time to exhaustion. Those studies used concentrated doses, often 300–1,000 mg of nitrate, taken a few hours before exercise.

Food First

  • Beets, roasted or juiced
  • Arugula and spinach
  • Celery, lettuce and Swiss chard

One extra helping of leafy greens a day is a simple, food-based way to get more nitrate.

Cautions

Beets can turn urine pink, which is harmless. People who take blood-pressure medication or nitrates for heart conditions should talk to their doctor before using concentrated nitrate supplements.
